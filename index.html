Milestones

Timeline, 1946 to 2026

1
The Constituent Assembly, 1946–1949

The constitutional question: where should democratic power reside?

The Draft Constitution of 1948 made no provision for local government. The Constituent Assembly debated the omission through November 1948 and settled on a Directive Principle.

The Drafting Committee designed a Republic with two levels of government, the Union and the provinces, and vested rights in individual citizens. Village self-government, central to Gandhi's vision, had no place in the draft. B.R. Ambedkar defended this choice when he moved the Draft Constitution for consideration on 4 November 1948.

A rural debate

In 1951, nearly 83% of Indians lived in villages. The Assembly's debate centred on panchayats, and towns came up mainly in discussions on local finance. No framework for municipal government was proposed.

Four positions in the Assembly

The individual as the unit

B.R. Ambedkar

Ambedkar's concern was the village as it existed, organised around caste and dominated by a few families. A Constitution that vested rights in individuals required local institutions that answered to equal citizenship.

Read alongside his defence of fundamental rights, the argument sets a condition for decentralisation: local institutions must protect the individual citizen.

Decentralisation as a safeguard

Damodar Swarup Seth and Arun Chandra Guha

Seth wanted India to be "a Union of small autonomous republic[s]" and argued that the only method of safeguarding against totalitarianism and fascism is that power should be decentralised to the greatest extent. Guha, speaking the next day, tied a strong Union to strong local institutions: We cannot have a strong Centre without strong limbs.

Seth, 5 Nov 1948, CAD Vol. VII pp. 212–213 · Guha, 6 Nov 1948, p. 256 · 5 Nov record · 6 Nov record

The risk of elite capture

Mono Mohan Das and N.G. Ranga

Das warned on 8 November that, without education and safeguards, panchayats would place power with zamindars, talukdars, mahajans and moneylenders (paraphrased). Ranga argued the next day that participation was the remedy, and that the State should set up panchayats in every village to help our villagers to gain training in self-government.

Das, 8 Nov 1948 (summary; transcription) · Ranga, 9 Nov 1948, CAD Vol. VII p. 351 · 9 Nov record

A long-term objective

M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar

Ayyangar supported a Constitution "based upon autonomous village republics" in principle, but saw no institutions ready to carry it: I agree this ought to be our objective. But where are these republics? They have to be brought into existence. K.T. Shah's proposal on 17 November to organise States as groups of village panchayats was negatived.

Ayyangar, 9 Nov 1948, CAD Vol. VII p. 352 · Shah, 17 Nov 1948 (transcription)

Article 40 and its limits

On 22 November 1948, K. Santhanam moved Draft Article 31-A, placing village panchayats among the Directive Principles. He left out conditions other members had sought, such as village self-sufficiency, because powers, area and functions would vary from province to province and it was "not desirable that any hard and fast direction should be given in the Constitution." Ambedkar accepted it in one sentence: Sir, I accept the amendment. The article was adopted the same day.

Article 37 makes Directive Principles unenforceable in court. Article 40 placed a duty on the State to organise panchayats, with no remedy where a State failed to act and no protection against dissolution. Satish Chandra, who had sought Fundamental Right status for panchayats, recorded in November 1949 that they had been placed among the Directive Principles instead.

Santhanam and Ambedkar, 22 Nov 1948, CAD Vol. VII p. 520 (record) · Satish Chandra, 24 Nov 1949, CAD Vol. XI (transcription)

Article 40, Constitution of India

"The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government."

Adopted as Draft Article 31-A on 22 November 1948. Under Article 37, Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court.

Municipalities in the debate

Municipalities entered the debate through their finances. R.K. Sidhwa of the Central Provinces described local bodies as "the pivots of the social and economic life of the country" and argued that provinces had taken over the taxes that should fund them.

In August 1949, the Assembly capped the tax on professions available to local bodies (now Article 276). Shibban Lal Saksena argued that local bodies were "practically starved of finances." Ambedkar's reply set out their constitutional position: the local authority is purely a creation of the State. Local government was placed in Entry 5 of the State List, leaving the existence, powers and finances of municipalities to each State legislature.

9 Aug 1949, debate on Draft Art. 256, CAD Vol. IX (reproduction; page not confirmed)

Town republics

On 5 November 1948, H.V. Kamath said the world would have to "decentralise and establish village republics and town republics." It is one of the few references to self-governing towns in the debates.

CAD Vol. VII pp. 219–220

The open question

If the Assembly accepted local self-government as a principle in 1948, why did panchayats and municipalities wait more than four decades for constitutional protection?

2
1950 to 1992

The long road to constitutional recognition

Constitutional status for local government took three Bills between 1989 and 1992. Each turned on the place of State governments in a three-tier system.

Article 40 led to Panchayati Raj legislation across States after the Balwantrai Mehta study team recommended democratic decentralisation in 1957. Municipalities remained creations of State law. State governments postponed municipal elections, superseded elected councils and transferred water supply, planning and housing to boards and development authorities accountable to the State.

Municipal finances weakened as cities grew. The Joint Committee that examined the urban Bill in 1992 recorded the trend.

Statement of Objects and Reasons, 1991

Local bodies had "become weak and ineffective" owing to "the failure to hold regular elections, prolonged supersessions and inadequate devolution of powers and functions."

Statement of Objects and Reasons, Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Bill, 1991, signed by Sheila Kaul (MoHUA text of the Act)

Three Bills, 1989 to 1992

  1. May to October 1989

    The 64th and 65th Amendment Bills, 1989

    Did not become law

    The Constitution (64th Amendment) Bill on panchayats was introduced on 15 May 1989, and the (65th Amendment) Bill on Nagarpalikas on 7 August. The Bills provided for five-year terms, elections within six months of a dissolution, reservations for SCs, STs and women, and finance commissions to review local finances. For cities, Rajiv Gandhi proposed directly elected Wards Committees in every city of one lakh or more.

    The Bills gave the Election Commission of India and the Comptroller and Auditor General a role in local governance. Opposition parties, several in power in States, saw this as a direct channel between the Union and local bodies that bypassed State governments. TDP's C. Madhava Reddi argued the panchayat Bill was "outside the competence of this House to legislate"; Syed Shahabuddin opposed the Nagarpalika Bill "not on merits but on constitutional grounds", citing the State List. The Bills were also introduced months before a general election.

    The Lok Sabha passed both Bills in August 1989. In October, each secured a majority in the Rajya Sabha but fell short of the two-thirds required for a constitutional amendment, at 158–83 and 157–83 according to press reports.

    Lok Sabha debates, 15 May 1989 and 7 August 1989; vote figures from a PTI report, not from the Rajya Sabha record. The Rajya Sabha debate of 16 August 1989, including Murasoli Maran's speech, could not be retrieved for this project, so his position is not characterised here.

  2. September 1990 to March 1991

    The National Front Bill, 1990

    Lapsed

    The V.P. Singh government introduced a single Bill covering panchayats and Nagarpalikas. Secondary accounts describe it as giving State legislatures more say over local powers. The government fell in November 1990, and the Bill lapsed without a vote when the Lok Sabha was dissolved in 1991.

    Business Advisory Committee, Lok Sabha, 1990, lists "The Constitution Amendment Bill, 1990 re: Panchayati Raj and Nagarpalikas" as a single Bill. Its official number could not be confirmed and is not given here.

  3. September 1991 to December 1992

    The 72nd and 73rd Amendment Bills, 1991

    Enacted

    On 16 September 1991, the P.V. Narasimha Rao government introduced the Constitution (72nd Amendment) Bill on panchayats, piloted by G. Venkatswamy, and the Constitution (73rd Amendment) Bill on municipalities, piloted by Sheila Kaul, Minister of Urban Development. Both were referred to Joint Committees of the two Houses in December 1991.

    Lok Sabha, 16 September 1991 and 20 December 1991

The Joint Committee on the urban Bill

Chaired by K.P. Singh Deo, the Joint Committee held 13 sittings, received 40 memoranda and 36 questionnaire responses, and heard oral evidence from ten witnesses. They included Prof. Abhijit Datta of the Indian Institute of Public Administration, K. Narayanaswamy of the All India Council of Mayors, K.C. Sivaramakrishnan of the National Institute of Urban Affairs and M.N. Buch of the National Centre for Human Settlement and Environment.

The committee strengthened the Bill's institutional design. It added District Planning Committees, and Metropolitan Planning Committees for India's 23 metropolitan cities, where development authorities were nominated bodies with no democratic base. It made Wards Committees mandatory in municipalities of three lakh or more, to bring large councils closer to residents. It also added a duty on the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to supplement municipal resources, and two new items to the Twelfth Schedule.

The committee also widened State discretion. Rotation of reserved seats was made optional, and the conduct of municipal elections was left to State law. Article 243W, under which a State legislature "may" endow municipalities with powers, was renumbered without change. The report gives no reason for retaining "may".

Report presented 14 July 1992: paras 1.5–1.9 (process), 1.20 (Wards Committees), 1.33–1.36 (planning committees), 1.39 (Art. 280), 1.40 (Twelfth Schedule), Appendix V (witnesses). Read the report.

The only minute of dissent to the urban report. Virumbi described the Twelfth Schedule as "a needless exercise" that repeated the State List, warned of friction between States and local bodies, and argued that devolution to cities required the Union to first share more powers and resources with States. The objection was federal, and the final text reflects it.

The rural Bill was examined by a separate Joint Committee chaired by Nathu Ram Mirdha. Its report could not be accessed for this project. According to the Minister's statement in the Lok Sabha on 1 December 1992, it recommended direct elections at every tier.

On 21 December 1992, the Lok Sabha voted 452 to 1 to take up the panchayat Bill. Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao, who also held the Rural Development portfolio, intervened briefly before the vote. The final texts replaced the 1989 roles of the Election Commission of India and the CAG with State Election Commissions and audit under State law. Why the numbers changed

Amendment Acts are numbered in the order they are enacted. The 71st (August 1992) and 72nd (December 1992) Amendments were enacted first, so the 72nd Amendment Bill, 1991 on panchayats became the 73rd Amendment Act, and the 73rd Amendment Bill, 1991 on municipalities became the 74th Amendment Act. Both received presidential assent on 20 April 1993.

3
In force from 1 June 1993

The 74th Amendment: what did India promise its cities?

Part IXA guaranteed the existence, composition and continuity of municipalities. Their powers, finances and staff were left to State legislatures.

The 73rd Amendment (Part IX, in force from 24 April 1993) covered rural panchayats, with a Gram Sabha and three tiers. The 74th Amendment (Part IXA, Articles 243P to 243ZG, and the Twelfth Schedule) covered urban areas. Both follow one design: mandatory elections and reservations, State Election Commissions and State Finance Commissions, with devolution of powers left to State law.

The diagram groups the urban provisions by the verb the Constitution uses. Provisions that say shall bind the States, and courts have enforced them. Provisions that say may leave the decision to the State legislature. Select an article to read what it provides.

Article 243W

Article 243W: "the Legislature of a State may, by law, endow the Municipalities with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self-government", including functions "in relation to the matters listed in the Twelfth Schedule."

The Twelfth Schedule lists matters a State legislature may assign to municipalities. It does not transfer any function by itself.

shallWhat the Constitution requires

Mandatory provisions: the existence, composition and continuity of municipalities.

243QEvery State must constitute municipalities

Three categories, as above. Before 1993, State law could govern a town without any elected body.

243RCouncils elected directly from wards

All seats are filled by direct election from territorial wards. State law may add MPs, MLAs and Wards Committee chairpersons, and nominated experts who cannot vote. The manner of electing the chairperson or mayor is left to the State.

243SWards Committees in cities of 3 lakh+

Compulsory in municipalities with a population of three lakh or more; a committee may cover one or more wards. Composition and territorial area are left to State law. The 1989 Bill, as Rajiv Gandhi described it, would have required them from one lakh.

243TReservation for SCs, STs and women

Seats for SCs and STs in proportion to population; not less than one-third of all seats for women. Rotation of reserved seats and reservation of chairpersons' posts are left to State law ("may").

243UA fixed five-year term

A municipality lasts five years. It must be heard before any dissolution, and a new election must be completed before the term ends or within six months of a dissolution. In Suresh Mahajan (2022) the Supreme Court called this mandate "inviolable".

243ZAState Election Commissions run the polls

Superintendence of electoral rolls and elections vests in the State Election Commission. Delimitation of wards is not assigned to it by the Constitution, and in many States the government retains that power.

243YA State Finance Commission every five years

The SFC reviews municipal finances and recommends how State taxes and grants should be shared. The Governor must lay its report before the legislature with a memorandum on action taken. The recommendations are not binding.

243ZDDistrict Planning Committees

In every district, to consolidate panchayat and municipal plans. At least four-fifths of members are elected from among elected local representatives, in proportion to the district's rural and urban population.

243ZEMetropolitan Planning Committees

In every metropolitan area (ten lakh or more), to prepare a draft development plan for the whole area. At least two-thirds of members are elected from among municipal councillors and panchayat chairpersons. The committee drafts plans; it has no executive power.

mayWhat it leaves to the State legislature

Enabling provisions: the powers, money and accountability of municipalities.

243WPowers, functions and responsibilities

The State legislature "may" endow municipalities with powers to function as institutions of self-government, including the preparation of plans and the performance of functions listed in the Twelfth Schedule, "subject to such conditions as may be specified" in its own law. The scope of a city government's powers is determined by each State.

243XTaxes and funds

The State "may" authorise municipalities to levy and collect taxes, duties, tolls and fees, assign them State taxes, and give grants. The Constitution guarantees municipalities no tax base of their own. The one explicit municipal tax, on professions, is capped by Article 276 at ₹2,500 a year.

243ZAudit of accounts

The State "may", by law, provide for maintenance and audit of municipal accounts. The 1991 Bill had provided for CAG audit of municipal corporations; the final text dropped it.

Not addressedStaff, mayors' powers and tenure, parastatals

Part IXA says nothing about who appoints and controls municipal staff, how long a mayor serves or what a mayor may decide, or how development authorities and water boards relate to the elected council. All of this sits in State law.

The constitutional settlement

The 74th Amendment guaranteed every city an elected council. The funds, functions and functionaries of that council were left to each State.

Evidence from ASICS 2023

Three decades later: how much of the promise has been kept?

Janaagraha's Annual Survey of India's City-Systems (ASICS) 2023 assessed urban laws in all 35 States and UTs. It describes implementation of the 74th Amendment as "a glass less than half-full."

ASICS 2023, the sixth edition of the survey, evaluates municipal acts, town and country planning acts and allied laws in 28 States and 7 Union Territories against 52 questions. The assessment period ran from December 2021 to December 2022, and the figures below refer to that period. Some have changed since; Mumbai elected a council in January 2026.

Most ASICS indicators assess what State laws provide. Figures that describe implementation are marked as such.

Functions

Municipalities able to plan and deliver the services in the Twelfth Schedule.

ASICS 2023 finds that the 74th Amendment "does not recognise 'city governments' as a distinct tier of government," and that six States devolve all 18 functions through "may" provisions that create no obligation. Where cities stand

Municipalities are now permanent, elected and more representative institutions. In most States, their funds, functions and functionaries remain under State control.

Outcomes vary widely across States. The CAG found that municipalities in Maharashtra and Jharkhand fully control ten functions, and those in Odisha and Uttarakhand none. Kerala and Maharashtra have Wards Committees in every audited municipality. State legislation and political choices account for much of this variation.

5
The unfinished promise

Five big shifts for city-systems reform

Janaagraha's 2026 report, Shaping Urban India: By Design, Not By Default, sets out the reforms India's cities need. State governments lead, with support from the Union government.

The report's central argument is that growth and liveability in Indian cities depend on each other, and that both are shaped by city-systems: planning and design, place-based empowered governance and state capacity. Its diagnosis matches the record of the 74th Amendment. Only about four of the 18 Twelfth Schedule functions have been effectively devolved across States. ULGs face a 42% gap between their revenues and their expenditure needs, with property tax rates set by State governments. Parastatals working in cities neither report to elected city governments nor include elected representatives on their boards.

The report holds that the constitutional framework for city government already exists. What is required is political commitment, institutional reform and sustained citizen engagement, with State governments creating the enabling conditions for reform now.

Shaping Urban India, pp. 9–11 (executive summary), 50–52 (diagnosis)

Constitution Day reflection

The Unfinished Promise of Local Self-Government

The Constituent Assembly accepted local self-government as a principle in 1948 and placed it among the Directive Principles. Parliament guaranteed elected councils in 1992, with fixed terms and reserved seats for women, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and left their powers to the States.

Thirty-three years on, councillors in most Indian cities have no authority over the water board, the development authority, municipal staff or the city's share of State revenue. Residents bring complaints about drainage and water supply to the councillor they elected, who often lacks the powers and funds to resolve them.

The 74th Amendment began the work of building city governments in India. Completing it means giving the institutions closest to citizens the funds, functions and functionaries to govern, with clear accountability for results. That is the unfinished constitutional task before India's cities.
